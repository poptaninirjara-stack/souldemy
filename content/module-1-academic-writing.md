# Module 1: Academic Writing — Why and How

**Track:** Research-Oriented English for Humanities (starter module; also feeds Communicative/Professional English)
**Who it's for:** First- and second-year BA students whose first language is not English
**Time-free module:** no timers, countdowns or time limits anywhere in this module.

## Module outcomes
By the end, a student can:
1. Say what academic writing is and why it is marked differently from everyday writing.
2. Decode an exam or assignment question (task words, parts, weighting).
3. Plan and shape an answer: introduction, body paragraphs, conclusion.
4. Write in a formal, precise, cautious academic style.
5. Follow a plan–write–check routine for exam answers.

## Lesson 1: What academic writing is

### Explain
Academic writing is writing that makes a **claim** and backs it with **evidence** and reasoning, for a reader who knows the subject and wants to see that you do too.

Three questions shape every piece:
- **Purpose:** what am I being asked to do (describe, explain, argue, evaluate)?
- **Audience:** who reads this? Usually a teacher or examiner who knows the topic but will not fill gaps for you. Explain anything essential, even if it feels obvious.
- **Register:** the level of formality. Academic register is formal, precise and cautious. It avoids slang and dramatic language.

### Same idea, three registers
| Register | Example |
|---|---|
| Casual | "Honestly, loads of people reckon social media is ruining kids' brains. I think they're right." |
| Journalistic | "Glued to their screens: is a distracted generation growing up?" |
| Academic | "Several studies associate heavy social media use with reduced attention among adolescents; however, the direction of cause remains disputed." |

Notice what the academic version does: it names its evidence type (studies), limits the claim ("associate", "among adolescents") and admits uncertainty.

### Exercise 1
Label each text **casual**, **journalistic** or **academic**, and underline one feature that tells you.
- (a) "A fresh study says old folk running firms do better than youngsters. What's their secret?"
- (b) "Firms founded by older entrepreneurs appear to survive longer than those founded by younger ones (Rao, 2019)."
- (c) "My uncle started a shop at 55 and it's still going. Age doesn't matter, obviously."

**Answer key**
- (a) Journalistic: question hook, "old folk", "youngsters".
- (b) Academic: cautious verb ("appear to"), reference in brackets, formal words.
- (c) Casual: personal anecdote as proof, "obviously", contraction.

*(The reference in (b) is invented for practice.)*

## Lesson 2: Why you need it

### Explain
Exams and assignments are not a test of how much you can remember. They test whether you can:
- understand the key ideas of a course,
- use those ideas to interpret a text, case or example,
- compare, connect and weigh different views,
- justify a judgement with evidence,
- think critically about the subject.

Marks go to reasoning shown on the page. A correct fact with no explanation earns less than a fact used to support a point. Beyond exams, the same skills are needed for seminar papers, projects, scholarship and job applications, and postgraduate study.

### Exercise 2
Each answer fragment shows one examiner expectation. Match it: **(i)** understands a concept, **(ii)** interprets with evidence, **(iii)** compares, **(iv)** justifies a judgement.
1. "Both poems treat war as loss, but the first blames leaders while the second mourns the soldiers."
2. "The term *diaspora* refers to a population dispersed from its original homeland while keeping ties to it."
3. "The repeated image of locked doors suggests the narrator feels trapped, since the setting never changes."
4. "This policy is weak because it improves access but ignores quality of teaching."

**Answer key:** 1 → (iii); 2 → (i); 3 → (ii); 4 → (iv).

### Reflect (not marked)
List three pieces of writing you will have to produce this year. For each, who is the reader and what are they looking for?

## Lesson 3: Reading the question

### Explain
Most lost marks come from answering a different question from the one set. Before writing, underline:
1. the **task words** (what to do),
2. the **topic** (what to do it to),
3. the **parts** (many questions ask for two things).

### Task words
| Group | Word | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Show what you know | **Define** | Give the meaning; sometimes more than one view of it |
| | **Describe / Outline** | Give the main features clearly |
| | **Explain** | Give reasons or show how something happens |
| | **Illustrate** | Give examples and show how each connects to the point |
| | **Summarise** | Cover the main ideas briefly |
| | **Trace** | Show how something changed over time |
| Show connections | **Compare** | Show similarities (and often differences) |
| | **Contrast** | Show differences |
| | **Relate** | Show how things are linked |
| | **Apply** | Use an idea to explain a specific case |
| Defend a position | **Analyse** | Break into parts and show how they work or why they matter |
| | **Evaluate / Assess** | Judge strengths and weaknesses, with reasons, and reach a conclusion |
| | **Discuss** | Look at different sides and reach a reasoned conclusion |
| | **Argue** | Take a side and defend it against the other |
| | **Justify / Prove** | Give reasons and evidence for why something holds |
| | **Synthesise** | Combine ideas into something new, with a reason for combining them |

### Weighting two-part questions
"Define X and evaluate Y" asks for two things. Give both a clear section. Usually the second, harder task deserves more space.

### Exercise 3
For each question, underline the task words and write the parts in a list.
- (a) Define "urbanisation" and outline its main effects on Indian cities.
- (b) Compare the portrayal of women in two novels you have studied.
- (c) Evaluate the claim that the printing press caused the Reformation.
- (d) Trace the growth of Indian nationalism between 1885 and 1947 and illustrate your answer with two key events.
- (e) "Memory is unreliable." Discuss with reference to psychological research.

**Answer key**
- (a) *Define*, *outline* → Part 1: meaning of urbanisation (short). Part 2: main effects (longer).
- (b) *Compare* → similarities and differences in how women are portrayed; two novels; give both equal attention.
- (c) *Evaluate* → weigh evidence for and against "caused"; conclude whether the claim holds.
- (d) *Trace*, *illustrate* → chronological development 1885–1947; two specific events as examples.
- (e) *Discuss* → arguments for and against unreliability, using psychological research as evidence; end with a judgement.

## Lesson 4: The shape of an answer

### Explain
Almost every academic answer follows **introduction → body → conclusion**. In an exam it is fine to have an obvious structure; clarity matters more than elegance.

**Introduction (usually short)**
1. Context or definition of key terms
2. Your **thesis**: a direct answer to the question
3. A **forecast**: the points you will make, in order

**Body paragraphs** (one point each)
- **Topic sentence:** the point of the paragraph
- **Evidence:** a fact, quotation, example or source
- **Explanation:** why the evidence supports your point
- **Link:** how it connects to your thesis or the next point

**Conclusion**
- Restate your answer in fresh words, say why it matters, add **no new evidence**.

### A good thesis answers the question and gives a reason
| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| "Social media has good and bad points." | "Social media widens access to political debate, but unequal access and misinformation limit its democratic value." |

### Exercise 4a: Put the sentences in order
These sentences form one paragraph. Put them in order and label the topic sentence.
- (a) Many households must walk long distances to reach a working water source.
- (b) Water scarcity shapes daily life in many drought-prone villages.
- (c) As a result, children, especially girls, often miss school to fetch water.
- (d) This burden falls unevenly because fetching water is usually assigned to women and children.

**Answer key:** b, a, d, c. (b) is the topic sentence. (a) gives detail, (d) explains who carries the burden, (c) gives the consequence.

### Exercise 4b: Write a thesis
Write a one-sentence thesis for: *Evaluate the claim that social media improves democratic participation.* It must answer the question and contain a "but", "although" or "while".

**Model answer:** "Social media lowers barriers to joining political debate, although the spread of misinformation weakens the quality of that participation."

## Lesson 5: Academic style

### Explain
Academic style is **formal, precise, impersonal and cautious**.

| Feature | Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | contractions (*don't*), slang (*kids*, *folks*) | full forms (*do not*), standard words (*children*) |
| Precise | *a lot of*, *things*, *and so on* | *a significant number of*, *factors*, name all items |
| Impersonal | *I think*, *you can see* | *It is widely argued that*, *The evidence suggests* |
| Cautious | *always*, *proves*, *obviously* | *often*, *suggests*, *tends to*, *may* |
| Objective | *sadly*, *luckily*, *shockingly* | state the facts and let them speak |

**On "I":** many humanities departments accept "I argue" for your own thesis. Follow your department's norm; when unsure, ask your teacher.

**Hedging:** claims like "education reduces crime" are rarely fully true. "Education tends to be associated with lower crime rates" is safer and more accurate.

### Three sentence errors that cost marks
| Error | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Fragment** | "Because the harvest failed." (incomplete thought) | "Because the harvest failed, prices rose." |
| **Dangling modifier** | "Walking into the hall, the speech had already begun." (the speech was not walking) | "Walking into the hall, I found that the speech had begun." |
| **Faulty parallelism** | "The course teaches students to read closely, writing clearly, and how to argue." | "…to read closely, write clearly, and argue." |

### Exercise 5a: Rewrite in academic style
1. Loads of people think the exam system is unfair.
2. Crime goes up when there are no jobs.
3. The writer doesn't really explain why.
4. Sadly, a lot of folk are getting poorer.
5. Poetry is the best way to show feelings.
6. Things got worse after the war.

**Model answers** (others are possible)
1. Many critics argue that the examination system is unfair.
2. Unemployment is often associated with higher crime rates.
3. The writer does not explain why.
4. Poverty appears to be increasing among a significant proportion of the population.
5. Poetry is often regarded as an effective means of expressing emotion.
6. Economic conditions deteriorated after the war.

### Exercise 5b: Fix the error
Correct each sentence and name the error.
1. Although the results were promising.
2. Opening the letter, the news shocked her.
3. The author is known for her clear style, sharp humour, and writing about rural life.

**Answer key**
1. Fragment → "Although the results were promising, further tests were needed."
2. Dangling modifier → "Opening the letter, she was shocked by the news."
3. Faulty parallelism → "…her clear style, sharp humour, and writing about rural life" becomes "her clear style, sharp humour and rural settings."

## Lesson 6: Exam workflow (plan, write, check)

### Explain
A reliable routine takes the panic out of exam answers. There is no clock in this lesson; the routine is the same whatever the paper.

**Step 1: Read the whole paper**
- Note how many marks each question carries; marks hint at length and effort.
- If there is a choice, read all options. Pick the questions where you can explain your answer in detail, not just the ones where you remember facts.

**Step 2: Decode the question** (Lesson 3)
- Underline task words, topic and parts. Number the parts.

**Step 3: Plan**
- Write a one-line thesis.
- List three or four points, each with one piece of evidence.
- Put the points in the order you will write them.
- For short answers, jot two or three ideas. For long answers, make a short outline.

**Step 4: Write**
- Start with the thesis and forecast.
- One point per paragraph, topic sentence first.
- Use key words from the question and from your course.
- Use signposts: *Firstly… In addition… However… Therefore…*
- Do not pad. Specific beats long.
- If a new idea comes while writing, note it on your plan and add it where it fits.
- If you are running short of space or pages, list the remaining points in note form so the examiner sees what you knew.

**Step 5: Check**
Use the checklist below.

### Final checklist
- [ ] Did I answer every part of the question?
- [ ] Does my conclusion match my thesis?
- [ ] Does every paragraph have a point, evidence and explanation?
- [ ] Is the style formal, precise and cautious?
- [ ] Have I removed repetition and padding?
- [ ] Are names, dates and terms correct?
- [ ] Is my handwriting readable, and are corrections neat (one line through errors)?

### Exercise 6: Build a plan
**Question:** *Evaluate the view that literature can change society.*

Illustrative notes (not in order):
- Dalit autobiographies brought caste discrimination into public debate.
- Many people never read serious literature.
- Premchand's stories drew attention to rural poverty.
- Censorship shows that governments think books have power.
- Literature may shape attitudes slowly rather than change laws.
- Reading habits vary by region and class.

Tasks:
1. Write a one-line thesis.
2. Sort the notes into "supports the view" and "limits the view".
3. Order your paragraphs.

**Model plan**
- **Thesis:** Literature can shift public attitudes and put hidden issues on the agenda, but its power to change society directly is limited by who reads it and how slowly attitudes change.
- **Supports:** Dalit autobiographies → caste in public debate. Premchand → rural poverty made visible. Censorship → states take books seriously.
- **Limits:** Many people never read serious literature. Reading habits vary by region and class. Attitudes shift slowly; laws change differently.
- **Order:** Intro → influence on attitudes (two examples) → censorship as evidence of power → limits of reach → limits of speed → conclusion (balanced judgement).

## Lesson 7: Checklist and practice

### Quiz (self-correcting, with explanations)
1. **Which task word asks you to weigh strengths and weaknesses and reach a judgement?**
   (a) Describe (b) Evaluate (c) Outline (d) Illustrate → **(b).** Evaluate requires a verdict backed by reasons.
2. **Which is the strongest thesis for "Discuss the impact of mobile phones on study"?**
   (a) "Mobile phones are everywhere."
   (b) "Mobile phones have good and bad effects."
   (c) "Mobile phones aid study through quick access to information, but constant notifications reduce sustained attention."
   (d) "I think phones are bad." → **(c).** It answers the question and gives both sides with reasons.
3. **Which sentence is academic in register?**
   (a) "Kids these days can't focus."
   (b) "Adolescents may find sustained attention harder when interrupted frequently."
   (c) "Obviously, phones wreck concentration." → **(b).** Formal, precise and cautious.
4. **A topic sentence should:**
   (a) give the evidence (b) state the point of the paragraph (c) summarise the essay (d) introduce new quotations → **(b).**
5. **The conclusion should not:**
   (a) restate the answer (b) say why it matters (c) introduce new evidence (d) match the thesis → **(c).**
6. **What is wrong with "Education always reduces crime"?**
   (a) Too informal (b) An absolute claim that is rarely fully true (c) Too short → **(b).** Use "tends to" or "is often associated with".
7. **"Define X and evaluate Y" means:**
   (a) Only define X
   (b) Only evaluate Y
   (c) Give both parts a clear section, with more depth to the evaluation → **(c).**
8. **If you run short of space in an exam, you should:**
   (a) stop writing
   (b) list remaining main points briefly in note form
   (c) repeat your introduction → **(b).** It shows the examiner what you knew.

### Practice answer (not timed)
Choose one question and write a full answer of about 250–300 words, using your plan.
- *Discuss the view that regional languages should be the main medium of instruction in schools.*
- *Evaluate the role of public libraries in a modern city.*

### Self-assessment rubric
Score each row: **Developing / Secure / Strong**.
| Criterion | Developing | Secure | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answers the question | Partly | All parts addressed | All parts, well weighted |
| Thesis and argument | Unclear | Clear answer | Clear, reasoned, balanced |
| Evidence | Little or vague | Relevant examples | Specific, explained, linked to thesis |
| Organisation | Hard to follow | Clear paragraphs | Signposted, logical flow |
| Style and accuracy | Informal, errors | Mostly formal and correct | Formal, precise, cautious, polished |
