## Module 1: Presentations and Seminars

### Why it matters
A seminar talk is a structured way of speaking. Your listeners cannot see the page, so they rely on your voice and your signals to know where you are in the talk.

### Core ideas
1. **Shape:** introduction, two to four main points, conclusion. A talk works much like an essay you speak aloud.
2. **Signposting:** short phrases that tell listeners where you are and what comes next. They help you stay organised too.
3. **Open with a map.** Say what you will cover and in what order, then follow that order.
4. **Slides support you; they don't replace you.** Keep text short and speak in full sentences.
5. **Voice:** speak a little slower than feels natural, pause after key points, and stress the words that carry meaning.
6. **Questions:** treat them as part of the talk. Prepare for two or three likely ones.

### Useful language

| Purpose | Spoken phrases |
|---|---|
| Opening | "Good morning, everyone. Today I'd like to talk about…" · "My talk has three parts." |
| Moving on | "That brings me to my second point." · "Now that we've seen X, let's turn to Y." |
| Examples | "For instance…" · "A good example of this is…" |
| Emphasis | "The key point here is…" · "What matters most is…" |
| Concluding | "To sum up…" · "In conclusion, I'd like to leave you with…" |
| Inviting questions | "I'd be happy to take questions." |
| Taking a question | "That's a fair question." · "If I understand correctly, you're asking…" |
| Not knowing | "I'm not sure, but I'll check and get back to you." |

**Written (slides):** one idea per slide, a short heading, three to five short points, no full paragraphs.

### Worked example (opening, 30 seconds)
"Good morning, everyone. I'm going to talk about how migration shapes cities. First, I'll explain why people move. Then I'll look at what changes in the city. Finally, I'll suggest what planners can learn. Please save your questions for the end."

### Marathi to English notes
- Marathi marks respect through verb forms (*tumhi*, *aapan*). English marks it through word choice, so "Excuse me, Professor, could I ask something?" already sounds respectful. You don't need "respected sir" before every sentence.
- *"मला एक शंका आहे"* is often translated as "I have a doubt." In a seminar, "I have a question" or "Could I ask a question?" is the usual choice.
- Marathi sentences often put the verb last, so speakers sometimes build a long sentence before giving the point. In English, state the main point first, then add detail.
- If you think in Marathi and translate while speaking, use short sentences. A short sentence with a pause is clearer than a long one with hesitation.

### Try it
1. Choose a topic from your own subject. Write a three-sentence opening that includes a map ("First… Then… Finally…") and say it aloud.
2. Add one signpost phrase between each pair of main points in a short talk you have already prepared.
3. Write one question your audience might ask, and practise answering it aloud in two or three sentences.

## Module 2: Professional Email

### Why it matters
For a teacher, internship coordinator or employer, an email is often the first impression of you. A clear email gets a faster, kinder reply.

### Core ideas
1. **Meaningful subject line:** it tells the reader what the message is about and helps them decide when to read it.
2. **Greeting:** open the way you would a letter, for example "Dear Dr. Jones," or "Dear Ms. Smith,".
3. **Standard spelling and punctuation.** No ALL CAPS and no text-message shortcuts such as "u".
4. **Short, direct paragraphs.** Say why you are writing in the first two sentences.
5. **Friendly but not jokey.** Jokes and witty remarks often don't land in email.
6. **Closing and name:** end with a sign-off ("Best," "Thanks," "Sincerely,") and your name. Add course or contact details when useful.
7. **Follow-ups:** once a conversation is going, you may drop the greeting. Reply within a reasonable time and trim old messages.
8. **Cautions:** don't send passwords or card details. Don't send an attachment to someone you don't know in the first email, unless they asked for it (for example, a job ad that requests a résumé). Double-check the To field before you hit Send, especially on group lists.

### Useful language

| Purpose | Written phrases |
|---|---|
| Opening | "I am a first-year BA student in your Psychology class." · "I am writing to ask about…" |
| Requesting | "Could you please let me know…?" · "I would be grateful if you could…" |
| Attaching | "I have attached my draft for your review." |
| Thanking | "Thank you for your time." |
| Closing | "I look forward to hearing from you." |
| Following up | "I'm following up on my email of 10 October." |

**Spoken link:** the same ideas apply to voice notes and calls. State your name and purpose first, then your question.

### Worked example
**Subject:** Request for a meeting about my Semester 2 project topic

Dear Professor Kulkarni,

I am a first-year BA Geography student in your Tuesday class. I would like to discuss my project topic before I submit my proposal.

Could we meet for ten minutes this week? I am free after 2 p.m. on Wednesday and Thursday.

Thank you for your time.

Best regards,
Aatma Shah
BA Geography, Year 1

*(Names are fictional.)*

**WhatsApp vs. email:** a WhatsApp message to a classmate can be short and casual. An email to a teacher follows the pattern above.

### Marathi to English notes
- *"Respected Sir/Madam"* is common in Indian English and is polite, but "Dear Professor Kulkarni" is the standard greeting in most professional settings.
- *"Kindly do the needful"* is widely understood in India but is vague. Say what you need: "Could you please confirm my attendance record?"
- *"Please revert"* is often used in India to mean "please reply." In many other English-speaking workplaces, "revert" suggests going back to an earlier state, so "please reply" is the safer choice.
- Marathi uses respectful plural forms (*tumhi*) with seniors. In English the respect comes from tone and structure, not from extra apologies.

### Try it
1. Rewrite this subject line: "Doubt." Make it say what the email is about.
2. Write a four-sentence email asking a teacher for an extension. Put your reason in the second sentence.
3. Take an old email or WhatsApp message you sent and rewrite it for a different reader: a teacher instead of a friend.

## Module 3: CVs, Cover Letters and LinkedIn

### Why it matters
These documents get you to the interview. Employers and internship coordinators often read them quickly, so clarity and relevance matter more than length.

### Core ideas
1. **Purpose:** a résumé summarises your education, experience and skills that are relevant to a specific job. Together with the cover letter, its job is to get you an interview.
2. **Tailor it.** Adjust each application to the role. Echo important words from the job description, and use active verbs.
3. **Cover letter:** introduces you and your résumé. It explains why you are writing, why you fit this role and organisation, and links your experience to what they need. Don't repeat the résumé line by line.
4. **Letter layout:** Purdue OWL's guidance suggests one page, single-spaced, 1-inch margins, the same font as your résumé, in business-letter format.
5. **LinkedIn is your CV online.** Use a clear, professional photo, a headline that says who you are and what you aim to do, an About section that tells your story, relevant skills, and a clean profile URL.
6. **Keep all three consistent.** Dates, titles and claims should match across CV, letter and profile.
7. **Early in your degree,** use coursework, projects, volunteering, college publications and societies as experience.

### Useful language

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| CV bullet (action verb first) | "Organised…" · "Edited…" · "Presented…" · "Coordinated…" · "Researched…" |
| Cover letter opening | "I am writing to apply for the position of… I was drawn to your work on…" |
| Linking skill to need | "My experience in… would help your team to…" |
| Closing | "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss…" |
| LinkedIn headline pattern | "BA English student | Editor | Interested in publishing and communications" |
| LinkedIn About opener | "I'm a second-year student who enjoys…" |

**Spoken link:** practise saying your LinkedIn About section aloud in 30 seconds. It becomes your introduction at networking events and interviews.

### Worked example (CV bullet, before → after)
- Before: "Responsible for college magazine."
- After: "Edited six articles for the college magazine and coordinated a team of four student writers."

*(Illustrative example. Use only claims that are true for you.)*

### Marathi to English notes
- Indian CVs sometimes include "Declaration" lines, father's name or photo. Practice varies by country and employer, so check the job posting or local norms.
- Avoid "Hardworking and sincere" on its own. Show it with one concrete example instead.
- *"Myself Aatma"* is a common pattern in Indian English introductions. In a cover letter or LinkedIn summary, write "I am Aatma" or start with what you do.
- Don't translate Marathi proverbs or idioms word for word into a cover letter. State the point plainly.

### Try it
1. Take one thing you did this year (a project, society role or part-time job). Write two CV bullets, each starting with an action verb.
2. Write a three-sentence cover letter opening for an advertised internship. Include the role name and one reason you fit.
3. Write a LinkedIn headline and a four-sentence About section. Read it aloud.

## Module 4: Interviews

### Why it matters
An interview is a spoken, unscripted conversation under mild pressure. Preparation gives you structure to lean on when nerves arrive.

### Core ideas
1. **"Tell me about yourself"** is usually the opening question. It is not a request for your life story.
2. **A simple structure:** present (what you are doing now), past (the experience that prepared you), future (why this role). Choose two to four points that match the job, and finish with one or two sentences on why you fit.
3. **Keep it short.** Aim for one to two minutes. Don't recite your CV.
4. **Behavioural questions** ("Tell me about a time when…") are best answered with **STAR**: Situation, Task, Action, Result.
5. **Prepare a few stories** (three to five) that can be adapted to different questions: teamwork, leadership, problem-solving, learning from a mistake.
6. **Say "I", not "we",** when describing your own actions, while still crediting your team.
7. **It is fine to pause.** Take a moment to think, or ask for clarification, before answering.
8. **No work experience yet?** Use class projects, group assignments, volunteering and college activities.
9. **Prepare one or two questions** to ask the interviewer at the end.

### Useful language

| Purpose | Spoken phrases |
|---|---|
| Starting | "Thank you for having me. I'm currently a second-year BA student in…" |
| Buying a moment | "That's a good question. Let me think for a moment." |
| Clarifying | "Could you repeat that, please?" · "Do you mean…?" |
| STAR: Situation | "In my second semester, I was part of a group project on…" |
| STAR: Task | "My responsibility was to…" |
| STAR: Action | "I decided to… I organised… I asked…" |
| STAR: Result | "As a result, we… What I learned was…" |
| Not knowing | "I haven't done that yet, but I've worked on something similar." |
| Closing | "Thank you for your time. I'm very interested in this role." |

**Written link:** after the interview, send a short thank-you email using the patterns from Module 2.

### Worked example ("Tell me about yourself", about 40 seconds)
"I'm a second-year BA English student, and I edit our college periodical. Over the past year I've led a small team and learned to meet deadlines and give feedback. I'm interested in this internship because it combines editing and communication, which is what I enjoy most."

*(Illustrative. Use only what is true for you.)*

### Marathi to English notes
- Marathi uses *"ek goshta sang"* (*"एक गोष्ट सांग"*), which is often rendered as "Tell me one thing." In an interview, "Let me add one point" or "May I add something?" is more natural.
- A *"What is your good name?"* style question is a literal translation of a polite form in Indian languages. In interviews, "Could you tell me your name?" is more usual.
- English often prefers the simple present or past for facts ("I study…", "I led…"), where Indian English may use the progressive ("I am having…", "I am studying since 2023"). Check this in your own speech.
- Fillers like *"mhanje"* are natural, but in English practise replacing them with a short pause or "I mean".

### Try it
1. Write and say aloud a 40-to-60-second answer to "Tell me about yourself" using present, past, future.
2. Choose one real experience (a project, society role or part-time job) and write four sentences using STAR. Say it aloud.
3. Ask a friend to read an interview question aloud, and answer without notes.
