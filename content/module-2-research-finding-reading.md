3. Turn a topic into a research question and a working search string.

## Lesson 1: What research is

### Explain
Research is a careful attempt to answer a **question** using **evidence**, and to present the answer as a reasoned **argument**. It is not collecting facts on a topic, and it is not copying what others say.

| | Topic | Research question |
|---|---|---|
| Example | "Partition in literature" | "How do two Partition short stories portray the loss of home, and what does this suggest about memory?" |
| What it gives you | A subject area | A direction, a method and an end point |

Scholars work in a **conversation**: others have already written about your topic. Good research reads that conversation, finds a point where something is missing, disputed or unclear (a **gap**), and adds to it.

**Summary vs research:** a summary says what a source says. Research uses sources to build and test your own answer.

### Exercise 1
Label each as **topic**, **fact**, or **research question**.
1. "The Guide was published in 1958."
2. "Colonial education in India."
3. "How does the narrator's use of flashback shape the reader's sympathy for the protagonist?"
4. "Women writers in Marathi."
5. "To what extent did print newspapers shape nationalist debate in nineteenth-century Bengal?"

**Answer key:** 1 fact; 2 topic; 3 research question; 4 topic; 5 research question.
*(Dates and titles are used as examples of the kind of statement, not as sources to cite.)*

### Exercise 2
Turn the topic "Colonial education in India" into a research question that contains a "how", "why" or "to what extent".
**Model answers:** "How did colonial English-medium schooling change the careers open to educated Indians?" / "To what extent did colonial education create a new reading public?"

## Lesson 2: Know your sources

### Explain
**Scholarly (academic) vs popular**
| | Scholarly | Popular |
|---|---|---|
| Written by | Experts, for other experts and students | Journalists or general writers, for the public |
| Checked by | Often **peer review**: other experts judge it before publication | General editors |
| Looks like | Abstract, references or works cited, structured argument | Headlines, images, no full reference list |
| Examples | Journal articles, university-press books | Newspapers, magazines, blogs |

**Peer review** means experts in the field evaluate an article before it is published. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not every scholarly source is peer-reviewed. Books are scholarly if they meet most of the criteria, but are rarely peer-reviewed in the same way.

**Primary vs secondary in literary and humanities study**
- **Primary:** the text or material you study, such as a novel, poem, letter, diary, speech or archive document.
- **Secondary:** writing that interprets or analyses primary material, such as criticism, biographies and scholarly articles.
- **Tertiary:** overviews, such as encyclopedias and textbooks. Use for orientation, not as your main evidence.

Popular sources can still be useful, for example as primary evidence of public opinion. Pick sources for the job you need them to do.

### Exercise 3
Classify each as **primary / secondary / tertiary**, and **scholarly / popular**.
1. A peer-reviewed journal article analysing imagery in a novel.
2. The novel itself.
3. A magazine feature on a famous author's life.
4. A subject encyclopedia entry on "Postcolonialism".
5. A soldier's wartime letter held in an archive.
6. A university-press book of essays on partition memory.

**Answer key**
1. Secondary, scholarly
2. Primary (literary text)
3. Secondary, popular
4. Tertiary, usually scholarly-reference but for orientation only
5. Primary
6. Secondary, scholarly

## Lesson 3: From topic to search

### Explain
Library databases and Google Scholar search by **keywords**, not full sentences. Build a search in four steps.

1. **Start from your research question.** Underline the main ideas.
2. **List keywords and synonyms** for each idea (for example *novel / fiction*, *memory / remembrance*).
3. **Join terms with Boolean operators.**
   - **AND** narrows: all terms must appear. (`partition AND memory`)
   - **OR** broadens: any term may appear; use for synonyms. (`novel OR fiction`)
   - **NOT** excludes. (`Mexico NOT city`)
4. **Use quotation marks** to keep phrases together (`"short story"`) and **brackets** to group (`(partition OR "1947") AND (memory OR remembrance)`).

Tips:
- Start with two or three strong terms; add or remove terms depending on how many results you get.
- Too many results: add a term with AND. Too few: add synonyms with OR or remove a term.
- Read the subject terms and author keywords on good results; reuse them.
- Databases differ slightly in syntax; check the "search tips" or help page.

### Exercise 4
Question: *How do Partition short stories portray memory of home?*
1. List two synonyms for each of: *Partition*, *short story*, *memory*, *home*.
2. Write one search string using AND, OR, quotation marks and brackets.
3. Your search returns 40,000 results. Name two ways to narrow it.

**Model answers**
1. Partition: *1947*, *division of India*; short story: *fiction*, *narrative*; memory: *remembrance*, *nostalgia*; home: *homeland*, *displacement*.
2. `("Partition" OR "1947") AND ("short story" OR fiction) AND (memory OR remembrance) AND (home OR homeland)`
3. Add another AND term (for example an author, region or language); use filters (date, subject, peer-reviewed); use a more specific phrase in quotation marks.

## Lesson 4: Where to look

### Explain
Use a mix of paid-for resources accessed through your institute and free open resources. **Your library will give you the exact login steps and tell you which services your institute has joined; check with them.**

| Resource | What it is | How you use it |
|---|---|---|
| **N-LIST (e-ShodhSindhu college component)** | National service by the INFLIBNET Centre giving eligible, registered colleges access to subscribed e-journals and e-books. | Log in through the N-LIST site (registered-member login) with the username and password your college helps you create; open the resource, then search and download. N-LIST also offers a **document delivery service** where college users can request articles they cannot access. |
| **DELNET (Developing Library Network)** | A resource-sharing network linking libraries across India. Offers union catalogues, a discovery portal, e-resources, **inter-library loan**, document delivery and reference services. | Your college library must be a member. Use DELNET to find which libraries hold a book or journal and to request it through your own library. |
| **Shodhganga** | INFLIBNET's open-access repository of Indian PhD theses. Free, no login; searchable by keyword, author, supervisor, university, subject and year. | Search for theses on your topic. Read the **literature review** and **bibliography** chapters: they give you a map of the field and a ready list of sources to trace. |
| **Google Scholar** | Free scholarly search engine. Boolean operators work. | Good for discovery; then check whether your institute can supply full text. |
| **Your own library's catalogue** | Books and journals held on campus | Ask the librarian for help with subject headings. |

### A simple research workflow
1. Write your research question.
2. Build a search string (Lesson 3).
3. Search an N-LIST resource, then Google Scholar, then Shodhganga.
4. Skim titles and abstracts; save the most relevant five to ten.
5. If full text is locked: try your institute's access, then request through document delivery (N-LIST) or inter-library loan (DELNET), or ask your librarian.
6. Record each source in your research log (Lesson 7).

### Exercise 5
For each situation, name the best first step.
1. You need a doctoral thesis on Marathi theatre and want it free, in full.
2. You found a journal article through Google Scholar but it is behind a paywall.
3. Your college library does not own a book that another library does.
4. You need a ready list of sources on a topic.

**Answer key**
1. Shodhganga.
2. Check your institute's access (N-LIST resources); if unavailable, request via document delivery or ask the librarian.
3. Ask the library to request it via inter-library loan (DELNET).
4. Read the literature review and bibliography of a recent thesis or survey article on your topic.

## Lesson 5: Reading a paper

### Explain
Do not start at the first word and read to the end. Read in **three passes**, each with a purpose. This approach is adapted for humanities articles from a widely used method for reading research papers.

**Pass 1: Get the gist**
- Read the title, abstract and introduction.
- Read the headings, then the conclusion.
- Glance at the works cited to see whose work the author uses.
- Decide: is this useful, and should I read on?

**Pass 2: Follow the argument**
- Read the whole paper with more care.
- Mark the **thesis**, each main point, and the evidence used (quotations, examples, data).
- Note quotations or sources you want to follow up.

**Pass 3: Test the argument**
- Ask whether the evidence supports the claims; look for assumptions, gaps and alternative readings.
- Note how this changes, supports or challenges your own question.

### Anatomy of a humanities article
| Part | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Title and abstract | Topic, approach, main claim |
| Introduction | Context, the **gap**, the thesis, the plan |
| Literature review | Who else has written on this; where this paper fits |
| Body sections | One point each, with evidence |
| Conclusion | Answer to the question; why it matters |
| Works cited | Sources to trace; evidence of research depth |

### Exercise 6: Pass 1 on an abstract
*(Abstract invented for practice.)*

"This article examines how three post-independence novels represent the village as a site of change. Existing criticism has treated rural settings mainly as backdrops. Drawing on close reading of narrative structure, I argue that the village functions as an active agent shaping character and plot. The article concludes that attention to setting reveals tensions between tradition and modernity that earlier readings overlook."

Answer in your own words:
1. What is the topic? 2. What gap does it name? 3. What is the thesis? 4. What method does it use? 5. Would you read on if your topic were "setting in Indian fiction"?

**Model answers:** 1. The village in three post-independence novels. 2. Criticism treats rural settings as backdrops. 3. The village is an active agent shaping character and plot. 4. Close reading of narrative structure. 5. Yes; it directly addresses setting and offers an argument and method you could use or challenge.

## Lesson 6: Reading critically

### Explain
Critical reading is reading to **question**, not to doubt everything. Ask what the writer claims, what supports it, and what it assumes.

**Four questions for any passage**
1. **Claim:** what is the writer trying to prove?
2. **Evidence:** what supports it (quotes, data, examples)?
3. **Assumption:** what must be true for the evidence to count?
4. **Limits:** what is left out, or what could be read differently?

**Annotate as you read:** underline claims, circle key terms, write short margin notes ("evidence?", "assumes…", "compare with…").

**Judge sources with the CRAAP test** (developed by librarians at California State University, Chico)
| Letter | Ask |
|---|---|
| **C**urrency | When was it published or updated? Does my topic need recent work? |
| **R**elevance | Does it answer my question? Is it at the right level? |
| **A**uthority | Who wrote it? What are their credentials or affiliation? |
| **A**ccuracy | Is it supported by evidence, reviewed, and checkable elsewhere? |
| **P**urpose | Why was it written: to inform, persuade, sell? Is bias clear? |

Note: older sources can still be authoritative in humanities (for example, a classic study). Currency matters more for fast-changing topics.

### Exercise 7: Analyse a passage
*(Passage invented for practice.)*

"Students who read novels regularly score higher in English exams. Therefore schools should make novel reading compulsory."

1. Claim? 2. Evidence? 3. Assumption? 4. Limit or weakness?

**Model answers**
1. Schools should make novel reading compulsory.
2. A reported link between novel reading and English exam scores.
3. That reading novels *causes* higher scores (not just that stronger students read more).
4. The link may be a correlation; no evidence shown that compulsion produces the same effect as voluntary reading.

### Exercise 8: Apply CRAAP
You find a blog post claiming a famous poem was "secretly" about a political event. It has no author name, no references and was posted ten years ago. List two CRAAP problems and what you would do next.
**Model answer:** *Authority:* no named author or credentials. *Accuracy:* no references or evidence. Next step: search for a peer-reviewed article or a reliable book on the poem and check whether the claim appears there.

## Lesson 7: Research log and practice

### Explain
Keep a log from the first source you read. It saves time later and prevents accidental plagiarism. For each source record:
| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Full citation details | Author, title, journal or book, year, volume/issue, pages, publisher, link or database |
| Type | Primary / secondary; scholarly / popular |
| Main claim | In one sentence, in your own words |
| Key evidence | What it uses to support the claim |
| Useful quote (with page number) | Short, exact, with page |
| My response | Agree, disagree, how it connects to my question |

The next module teaches paraphrasing, summarising and referencing in full, using these notes.

### Practice (not timed)
1. Choose a topic from your course and write a research question.
2. Build a search string.
3. Find three sources (one primary, two secondary) using the resources in Lesson 4.
4. Do pass 1 on each; do pass 2 on the best one.
5. Add all three to your research log.

### Quiz (self-correcting, with explanations)
1. **Which is a research question?**
   (a) "Gender in Indian fiction" (b) "Premchand wrote about villages" (c) "How does Premchand's use of dialogue shape the reader's view of rural poverty?" → **(c).** It asks "how" and sets a clear direction.
2. **A novel you are analysing is a:**
   (a) primary source (b) secondary source (c) tertiary source → **(a).**
3. **Which Boolean operator broadens a search?**
   (a) AND (b) OR (c) NOT → **(b).** OR returns results with any of the terms; use it for synonyms.
4. **You get 80,000 results. What helps most?**
   (a) Remove all keywords (b) Add a specific term with AND or apply filters (c) Use OR more → **(b).**
5. **Where can you read full Indian PhD theses free, without login?**
   (a) Shodhganga (b) A newspaper site (c) A blog → **(a).**
6. **What is DELNET most useful for?**
   (a) Writing citations (b) Sharing library resources, including inter-library loan (c) Plagiarism checking → **(b).**
7. **In pass 1, you should:**
   (a) Read every word (b) Read the title, abstract, introduction, headings and conclusion (c) Read only the works cited → **(b).**
8. **"Studies show that X, so everyone must do X" hides which problem?**
   (a) A grammar error (b) An unstated assumption linking evidence to the conclusion (c) A formatting error → **(b).**
